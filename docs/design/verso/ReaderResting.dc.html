Middlemarch

Mr. Brooke had been to the county town and come back with a cold and a great many opinions, and it was the opinions that kept him in his study all the following day, for a cold may be nursed in any chair but an opinion wants a desk and a fire and somebody to disagree with.

Celia, who had no opinions she could not set down at a moment's notice, sat with her work by the window and watched the gardener's boy carry leaves from one side of the lawn to the other. It seemed to her a very reasonable occupation. Her sister would have found a meaning in it, and been unhappy about the meaning; Celia found only that the boy whistled out of tune, and that the leaves, wherever he put them, blew back.

“Dodo will be home by four,” she said, to nobody.

Old and Young

Nobody answered, which was usual, and the clock on the landing, which was always a little ahead of the house, struck a quarter past three as though to prove her wrong.

In the study Mr. Brooke had arrived, by way of the poor law and the price of hay, at the general proposition that a man ought to look into things; and having looked into several, and found them all much as he had left them, he rang for more coals and began a letter to the newspaper which he did not intend to send.

It is a comfortable thing, a letter one does not intend to send. It may say anything at all.
