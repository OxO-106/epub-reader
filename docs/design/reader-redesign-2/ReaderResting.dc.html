and the lane, which she had always thought of as leading somewhere, turned out on closer acquaintance to lead mostly to itself, curling back past the mill pond and the row of pollarded willows as if it had forgotten its errand.

She laughed, alone there, and the sound surprised her. It had been some weeks since she had laughed at anything that was not meant to be laughed at; and she found, a little to her shame, that the lane had done her more good than the sermon.

A wren went in and out of the hedge ahead of her, keeping always the same distance, like a small and very busy guide who did not care to be thanked.

By the time she reached the gate the clouds had begun to break, and long bars of light lay across the meadow in the order of a ruled page. She stood with her hand on the latch and read them, or tried to, as one reads a letter in a hand one does not yet know.

Inside, someone was playing the piano, badly and with great feeling, and she waited until the piece was done before she went in.
